Improving Mobile Money Payment-Confirmation Interfaces

Understanding how merchants verify Mobile Money payments in Rwanda and exploring opportunities to make payment confirmation safer, more usable, and more privacy-conscious.

The Research

A security problem hiding in an everyday interaction

Mobile Money has become an important part of everyday financial life in Rwanda. Merchants frequently need to verify whether a customer's payment has actually been completed.

In many situations, merchants rely on the customer's phone or payment confirmation to make this decision. What appears to be a simple interaction raises important questions about security, privacy, trust, and usability.

Our research investigates these real-world payment confirmation practices and explores how these interactions can be improved.

Why Rwanda?

Some of the most interesting cybersecurity questions emerge from the technologies people use every day. By studying Mobile Money practices in Rwanda, we can better understand security challenges in a context that is often underrepresented in the global security literature.

The Journey

From an everyday observation to USENIX Security

The journey was much more than writing a paper. It involved understanding real users, questioning assumptions, iterating on ideas, and learning how to communicate research clearly.

01 — Identify the problem

We started by examining how merchants and customers actually interact when verifying Mobile Money payments.

02 — Listen to users

We engaged with people in real-world settings to understand their experiences, practices, challenges, and expectations.

03 — Analyze the evidence

Instead of relying on assumptions, we used the evidence collected through the research to understand the problem.

04 — Develop and refine our ideas

We explored how payment-confirmation experiences could be improved while keeping usability and real-world constraints in mind.

05 — Write, revise, and submit

The work went through many rounds of writing, discussion, feedback, revision, and refinement.

06 — USENIX Security '26

The research was accepted to the 35th USENIX Security Symposium and later received a Distinguished Paper Award.

What We Learned

Security is more than technology

One of the biggest lessons from this research was that secure systems must account for the people who use them and the environments in which they operate.

01

Real-world workflows matter

Security mechanisms need to fit the practical realities of the people using them.

02

Assumptions are not evidence

Research requires listening carefully and allowing evidence to challenge what we initially believe.

03

Usability is security

A security mechanism is less effective when it is too difficult or disruptive for people to use.

04

Context matters

Security challenges can look very different depending on the social and technological environment.

05

Research is iterative

Good research evolves through questioning, feedback, experimentation, and repeated refinement.

06

People are at the center

Ultimately, cybersecurity is about protecting people, their information, their money, and their trust.

The People Behind the Work

The SUPERgroup

I am equally thankful to the entire SUPERgroup. Every brainstorming session, paper review, discussion, critique, and suggestion helped make this work stronger. Being part of a research community where ideas are challenged constructively has been one of the most valuable parts of this journey.

Personal Reflection

What this project taught me

This project changed the way I think about cybersecurity research.

Great security research begins by understanding real people.

I learned that research requires humility. We have to be willing to question our assumptions, listen to users, accept criticism, and continuously improve our ideas.

I also learned that writing is part of research. Every revision forced us to clarify our thinking and communicate our contribution more precisely.

Most importantly, this experience strengthened my belief that important cybersecurity research can emerge from Africa and contribute to the global security community.
